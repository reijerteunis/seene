# An Outcome cannot count its own review rounds

The harness requires a ticket's `## Outcome` to be written before the review stage gate, because the reviewed-tree fingerprint covers the whole ticket file and anything added afterwards makes `verify-delivery` refuse. So the Outcome is written before the review that decides whether another round is needed. Any statement in it about how many rounds the ticket took is therefore a claim about the future at the moment it is written: it can only be true if the review it is about to face passes. A ticket that needs one more round has falsified its own Outcome by the act of being reviewed, and the sentence that says so most precisely is the one that goes stale first.

SEEN-106 established this the expensive way. Findings F9, F10, F11, F12, F13, F16 and F17 on that ticket were all one defect, each found by a separate review round, and not one of them touched the built behaviour: a stale opening summary (F9), a cross-reference to a criterion an amendment had moved (F10), a closing note whose counts contradicted the opening four lines above it (F11), four attributions of an act to an attempt ordinal the journal numbers differently (F12), the counts themselves (F13), a count of the reviews that found the counts (F16), and a superlative written to replace a count (F17). Some were introduced by the fix for the one before. F16 sat one paragraph from F13's fix and survived it; F17 *was* F13's fix.

The grammatical form does not matter, which is the part that took longest to see. A count ("seven attempts, six returns") is stale after the next round. An ordinal ("at the fourth attempt's solution stage") requires the reader to reconstruct a number the journal already assigns, and the writer kept getting it wrong. A comparison or a superlative ("more rounds of review than any other ticket in this epic") is worse than either, because it can be false on the day it is written as well as stale afterwards, and that one was: another ticket in the same epic had more returns on the day the sentence was written. All four forms are the same self-reference.

## Consequences

An Outcome states what was built, the evidence for it by record number, and what moved to another ticket and why. It does not count, order, compare or characterise the ticket's own attempts, returns, review rounds or records. Where a reader wants those, the Outcome points at `docs/harness/history/<ticket>/`, which is the only place they are correct by construction, because it is the thing they are counts of.

Two kinds of number remain legitimate in an Outcome and should not be confused with the above. Figures about the work are stable and belong there: `slice_points` from the solution records, the criteria delivered, the mechanisms built. Figures about a past, closed act are also stable, such as a count of sentences a named finding corrected. The test is whether the review now beginning could make the sentence false. If it could, the sentence is about the process rather than the work, and the journal owns it.

An amendment is where this defect breeds, because an amendment moves what the summaries summarise. When a criterion moves, a status changes or a scope narrows, the authoritative statements get updated and the summaries are what get left behind. SEEN-106's F9 and F10 were exactly that. After any amendment, re-audit every summary and cross-reference mechanically rather than by reading, and cover comparatives and superlatives as well as counts and ordinals: an audit for one form silently passes the others, which is how F16 and F17 both survived an audit that claimed to be exhaustive.

The reviewer cannot be relied on to catch these cheaply. Every one of those rounds cost a return, a regression run against the corrected tree, a fresh triage and a fresh review, and one produced a finding that did not hold at all, quoting two records as one. Writing an Outcome that cannot go stale is cheaper than finding out round after round that it did.
